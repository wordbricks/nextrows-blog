December closed the year with a new flagship model, a landmark law and a landmark lawsuit. All three were about the same thing: what goes into AI, and who gets a say.

## Google launches Gemini (December 6)

Google introduced Gemini, its largest and most capable model, built from the ground up to be multimodal and offered in three sizes: Ultra, Pro and Nano. Bard was upgraded to run on Gemini Pro immediately, and Google said Gemini Ultra was the first model to outperform human experts on the MMLU benchmark. After a year of playing catch-up, Google had a flagship again.

## The EU strikes a deal on the AI Act (December 8)

After three days of marathon negotiations, the European Parliament and Council reached a provisional agreement on the AI Act, the world's first wide-ranging AI law. The deal banned practices like social scoring and most real-time biometric identification, and set new obligations for powerful general-purpose models, with phased enforcement to follow. The regulation debate that started the year ended it with actual text.

## The New York Times sues OpenAI and Microsoft (December 27)

The Times filed a federal lawsuit alleging that OpenAI and Microsoft used millions of its articles to train their models without permission, seeking damages it said could reach billions of dollars. It was the first major US news publisher to take the AI labs to court, after months in which others chose licensing deals instead. The training-data fight now had a courtroom.

## Why this month matters if you work with web data

December's three stories share one thread: data - who made it, who may use it, and in what form. Teams that treat web data as a structured, accountable asset rather than a free-for-all are who NextRows builds for.
