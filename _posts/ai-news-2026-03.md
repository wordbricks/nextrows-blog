March was not a model month. It was the month AI companies, governments and courts started drawing hard lines around each other.

## Anthropic sues the US government (March 9)

After months of negotiations over how Claude could be used by the military, the Pentagon formally designated Anthropic a "supply-chain risk" - a label usually reserved for foreign firms - and President Trump ordered the government to stop using Claude. The Pentagon insisted on "any lawful use" of the technology; Anthropic refused to drop its usage restrictions and sued, filing a second lawsuit the same day over a broader designation that could blacklist it across the civilian government. Per Reuters, the fallout was immediate: Anthropic's chief commercial officer said one partner had already switched away from Claude, eliminating an anticipated revenue pipeline of more than $100 million.

## The UK backtracks on AI and copyright (March 18)

The UK government abandoned its proposal to let AI companies train on copyrighted works unless creators opted out, after a consultation drew overwhelming opposition - Elton John, Dua Lipa and Paul McCartney among the critics. "We have listened," Technology Secretary Liz Kendall said, announcing the government no longer favours the opt-out approach. For anyone whose business reads the web, the signal was clear: the rules for using other people's data are still being written, and consent is gaining ground.

## OpenAI closes Sora (March 25)

OpenAI shut down Sora, its headline video-generation app, and wound down its $1 billion content partnership with Disney, as the BBC first understood it - a pullback from consumer spectacle toward professional tools as IPO preparations loomed.

## Why this month matters if you work with web data

March put data rights and usage terms on the front page. Extraction businesses live and die by what they are allowed to read and how they may use it - and that is now a boardroom and courtroom question, not a footnote.
