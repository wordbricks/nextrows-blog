April pushed generative AI into two new arenas: open-source models that companies could actually build businesses on, and synthetic media good enough to fool the music industry.

## Meta open-sources Segment Anything (April 5)

Meta released the Segment Anything Model, a promptable foundation model for image segmentation, alongside SA-1B, a dataset of 1.1 billion segmentation masks across 11 million images. The claim was significant: segmentation - traditionally a model per problem - became a zero-shot capability you simply point at, the way text became promptable before it.

## Databricks ships Dolly 2.0 (April 12)

Databricks released Dolly 2.0, a 12-billion-parameter instruction-following model trained on 15,000 instruction records written by its own employees, and licensed the whole package for commercial use. At a time when the strongest open models traced back to Meta's research-only LLaMA, Databricks billed Dolly 2.0 as the first open, instruction-tuned LLM that businesses could use without a legal gray zone.

## An AI 'Drake' song gets pulled (April 18)

"Heart on My Sleeve," a track built by an anonymous creator using AI-cloned vocals of Drake and The Weeknd, racked up millions of plays before being pulled from TikTok, Spotify, YouTube and Apple Music after Universal Music Group objected. The takedown turned AI voice cloning from a novelty into the year's defining copyright fight, with labels demanding that platforms block AI training on their catalogs.

## Why this month matters if you work with web data

April's theme was provenance: where a model's pixels, weights and vocals come from. Provenance questions are data questions, and they start with clean, structured records - the output NextRows is built to produce.
