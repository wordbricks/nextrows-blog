May was the month AI stopped being only a product story. The field's most decorated researcher walked out to warn about it, and its most visible CEO flew to Washington to ask for rules.

## Geoffrey Hinton quits Google to warn about AI (May 1)

The New York Times reported that Geoffrey Hinton, whose work underpins modern deep learning, had left Google after a decade so he could speak freely about the risks of the technology: floods of convincing fake content, upended job markets, and longer-term threats he said he had not expected so soon. Hinton told the paper he partly regretted his life's work, while stressing that Google itself had acted responsibly.

## Google puts PaLM 2 across its products (May 10)

At Google I/O, the company introduced PaLM 2, its next-generation language model, and said it already powered more than 25 products and features, from Bard to Workspace. Google also removed Bard's waitlist and opened it in more than 180 countries and territories. The message of the keynote was scale: AI in everything, everywhere, at once.

## Altman asks the Senate to regulate AI (May 16)

In his first appearance before Congress, OpenAI CEO Sam Altman told a Senate Judiciary subcommittee that government intervention would be needed to manage the risks of increasingly powerful models, floating ideas like licensing requirements and a new agency for the largest systems. An AI CEO asking to be regulated was itself the headline, and the hearing set the template for a year of AI policy debate.

## Why this month matters if you work with web data

May moved AI from product news to public policy. As regulators start asking what went into a model and what comes out, teams need trustworthy, structured data pipelines - exactly the job NextRows takes on.
